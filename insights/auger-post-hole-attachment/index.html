Post-Hole Augers on Skid Steers: Flow, Torque and Technique

What the auxiliary circuit really delivers, how flow converts into bit speed, and the drilling sequence that keeps fence-line holes vertical.

Published November 28, 2026

A post-hole auger is the cheapest way to turn a compact loader into a fence-line machine, and one of the quickest ways to destroy a pump. The failures trace to three causes: a drive motor chosen from a catalogue rather than from the flow at the couplers, a relief setting above what the circuit is rated for, and an operator who uses the lift arms as a drill feed.

Quick answer: measure auxiliary flow at the couplers at operating temperature, then pick a motor giving roughly 60–120 rpm there for cohesive soil. Keep downforce within what the arms can apply without unloading the front axle, and drill in 200–300 mm bites.

What the auxiliary circuit delivers

Flow in litres per minute sets rotation speed; pressure in bar sets torque. Neither is the number on the loader's sales sheet, which quotes pump displacement at rated engine speed with no circuit losses.

Expect 10–20% less flow at the couplers than at the pump, and more with worn quick couplers. A flow meter gives the figure directly; a bucket and a stopwatch come close enough for attachment selection — 20 litres in 24 seconds is 50 L/min. Read it at operating oil temperature, at the speed you will work at, through the coupler pair you will use. Circuit behaviour under load is covered in our guide to skid steer attachment hydraulic flow.

Pressure needs the same care: the relief setting is in the manual and the motor must be rated for it. If a blocked auger makes the engine labour instead of opening the relief, stop — full pressure with nowhere to go finds the weakest hose. A latching auxiliary matters too; without one a second person holds the control, which fails when that person walks away.

From flow to bit speed

Auger speed follows from flow and motor displacement: rpm = flow (L/min) × 1000 ÷ displacement (cm³/rev). Torque follows from pressure and displacement, less mechanical efficiency — around 0.9 for a gear or piston motor in this size range.

In cohesive soil, 60–120 rpm cuts cleanly and lets the flighting lift spoil. Small diameters in dry sand tolerate 150–250 rpm because the hole clears faster than it collapses. Above roughly 300 rpm the flighting throws spoil instead of lifting it and the drive overheats.

Torque, diameter and soil: where augers stall

Torque demand rises faster than diameter: 250 mm to 600 mm more than doubles the cutting radius and multiplies spoil volume per revolution, so a motor that spins a 250 mm flight easily may stall on a 600 mm one in the same clay. Damp sand flows back into the hole; gravel catches the edge and shocks the drive.

That is why attachment charts quote torque per diameter and per ground condition, and why a drive is sized against the hardest ground on the job. Rock needs a rock bit, low speed and steady downforce; most compact loaders are the wrong carrier.

Downforce: what the lift arms can and cannot give

A skid steer lift circuit is a lifting device, not a drill feed. Pushing with the arms transfers weight off the front axle, which is exactly when a loader tips forward. The flighting pulls itself in; the arms only keep the drive aligned.

The sequence that keeps holes vertical

  • Spot and level. Square the machine to the line and level the frame: 2° off at the start is visibly off at 800 mm.
  • Start slow. Engage at low engine speed until the pilot bit bites, then build up; full flow from the start makes the auger walk across hard ground.
  • Bite, then clear. Drill 200–300 mm, lift clear with the auxiliary turning to throw spoil, then return. In clay this stops the flighting packing solid; in sand it lets the hole stand.
  • Check verticality as you go. Stones and roots push an auger off line and it does not straighten itself.
  • Never pry while turning. Hands, feet and levers stay clear until the auxiliary is off and the drive has stopped.

Most non-hydraulic problems are mechanical: confirm the coupler locks with its safety pin in, route hoses clear of the lift arm, and wipe the couplers before connection, because grit ends up in the motor. Carry spare shear pins of the specified grade. Our skid steer attachments range covers matching auger drives, with the mounting plate and hose set confirmed per machine.

Frequently asked questions

Can any skid steer loader run a post-hole auger?

Only if the auxiliary circuit has enough measured flow and a pressure rating the motor is built for. A small auxiliary pump turns a 200 mm auger slowly and stalls a 400 mm one. Measure at the couplers and confirm the relief setting before choosing a drive.

What is the largest auger diameter my loader can turn?

There is no single number: torque demand rises steeply with diameter and soil strength, and a 600 mm flight in stiff clay can need several times the torque of a 250 mm one. Work up from the diameter your soil needs and treat repeated stalling as the limit.

Why does the auger stop as soon as it meets a root?

The circuit has reached relief, or a shear pin has done its job. Stop the flow, reverse briefly to clear the flighting, and lift out to remove the obstruction. Holding the auxiliary on relief overheats the oil in seconds.
