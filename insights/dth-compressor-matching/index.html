Matching Your Compressor to a DTH Water Well Rig

How to match compressor pressure and air volume to a DTH water well rig: hammer size, hole diameter, elevation, hose losses and published model data.

Published November 22, 2026

A down-the-hole (DTH) hammer only drills at the rate its datasheet promises if the compressor behind it delivers the right pressure and volume at the hole. Compressor matching is a system decision: hammer size, hole diameter, rod size, elevation and hose layout all change the requirement. This guide explains what our published air figures mean, where the air is lost before it reaches the bit, and what to send us so the compressor and the water well rig arrive matched.

Quick answer: Match the compressor to the hammer first, then confirm the rig datasheet covers that pressure and volume. Pressure decides whether the hammer fires with full blow energy; volume decides whether the annulus stays clean. One cannot compensate for the other.

Pressure and volume do two different jobs

The hammer piston is driven by pressure. A hammer rated for 2.4 MPa running on a compressor set at 1.7 MPa loses blow energy on every stroke, and the loss shows up as slower penetration and higher bit wear rather than as an obvious fault. Volume does a different job entirely: it sets the annular velocity that carries cuttings past the hammer and out of the hole. When the volume is too low the hole loads up, the bit regrinds the cuttings it has already made, and the rate of penetration falls even though the hammer is firing normally.

So "how many CFM do I need" is not a complete question. The useful version is: what pressure and what volume must hold at the compressor outlet, at this hole diameter, at this elevation, with this hammer.

Where the air is lost before it reaches the hammer

A compressor rated at 24 m³/min free air delivery does not deliver 24 m³/min at the hammer. Losses accumulate along the route:

  • Elevation. The same compressor produces less mass flow at 1,800 m above sea level than at sea level for the same gauge pressure, because the intake air is thinner. State the site elevation in the enquiry.
  • Hose and pipe length. Every metre of hose, every bend and every undersized coupling adds pressure drop.
  • Tool joints and string geometry. Long rod strings with several joints add restriction — one reason the larger models publish higher drill-pipe diameters, 102 to 114 mm on the FY500 and above.
  • Back pressure from cuttings and water. As the annulus fills with cuttings, or when the hole takes on water, the pressure the compressor must overcome rises. Falling penetration with a steady gauge reading usually points here.

The practical consequence: size the compressor with margin above the rig's published consumption figure, not exactly at it.

Match the hammer to the hole, then the compressor to the hammer

Hammer selection comes before compressor selection, because the hammer's rated pressure is the floor for the compressor setting. Work in this order:

  1. Fix the final hole diameter and the casing plan, because they decide the hammer size.
  2. Take the hammer's rated working pressure as the minimum compressor pressure, and its rated air consumption as the minimum volume at that pressure.
  3. Add the loss allowance for elevation, hose length, string depth and expected water inflow.
  4. Check the result still sits inside the rig's published range for that diameter. If it does not, the rig and compressor are not yet a matched pair.

If your formation mixes unconsolidated overburden with hard rock, compare the air and mud cases before committing. Frequently asked questions

Can I run a DTH water well rig on a lower-pressure compressor?

Only within the hammer's rated range. A hammer designed for 2.4 MPa will not deliver its catalog blow energy at 1.7 MPa, and penetration rate usually falls faster than the pressure drop suggests. If the compressor you already own is low pressure, tell us the model and we will confirm which hammer sizes and hole diameters it can realistically support.

Does more air volume always mean faster drilling?

No. Volume has to be enough to lift cuttings at the annular velocity your hole diameter and rod size produce. Beyond that point, extra volume mostly adds fuel burn and dust. Correct bailing velocity depends on hole diameter, rod diameter and the density of the cuttings, so a larger hole needs a larger volume for the same cleaning effect.

Which compressor suits the FY300A / FY300B?

The FY300A and FY300B datasheets list 1.7-3.4 MPa working air pressure and 17-36 m3/min air consumption for a 140-325 mm hole. That is the rig's operating range, not a single required compressor size. Send the target hole diameter, hole depth, elevation and the hammer you intend to run, and we will state the minimum pressure and volume in the written quotation.
