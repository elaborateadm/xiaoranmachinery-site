Man-Portable Drilling: When Trucks Cannot Reach the Site

How man-portable and walk-in core rigs are specified when terrain rules out trucked rigs — access limits, module handling, lifting and rig classes.

Published November 26, 2026

Man-portable drilling is the option that stays open when the ground, the access road or the site itself rules out a truck-mounted or crawler rig. It is not one machine but a way of breaking a core drilling system down until every part can be carried, winched or lifted to the collar. Getting the decision right means looking at module weights, access geometry and how the crew will move rods, casing and water — not only at a rig’s headline depth.

What "man-portable" really covers

Two different setups are sold under the same label. The first is a compact rig that can be walked or winched in on its own skids or tracks and set up without a crane. The second is a genuinely hand-carried system, broken into modules light enough for a crew to move along a trail, up a slope or through a doorway where no vehicle will pass. The distinction matters because the productivity is very different. A walk-in crawler rig keeps the normal drilling cycle intact; a hand-carried rig trades productivity for access, and the labour of moving it becomes part of the daily plan rather than a one-off mobilisation.

Where tracks can reach the collar, a compact crawler core rig such as the FDX200 — 260 m BTW and 200 m NTW — usually out-drills a hand-carried alternative across a full program, because its rod handling, feed and wireline winch are sized for continuous work. Man-portable only wins when access, not depth, is the binding constraint.

Access limits that rule out trucked rigs

Trucked rigs fail on access long before they fail on depth. The usual blockers are forest-road width against the carrier, bridge and culvert load limits, gradient and switchback radius on mountain roads, overhead clearance under power lines and canopy, and ground bearing on soft or waterlogged approaches. Each one removes a class of machine, so the access survey should be done before a rig is chosen, not after.

Getting modules to the collar: carries, winches and lifts

Carrying is the cheapest move and the slowest. It suits short distances, gentle grades and light modules, and it sets a hard daily ceiling on how much casing, water and rod the crew can keep at the collar. For modules too heavy to carry and too remote to drive to, the options are a winch line run from a solid anchor, a small skid-mounted hoist, or a compact crawler crane narrow enough to use the same access as the rig.

On projects that run drilling and lifting on the same restricted site, buyers often standardise on one supplier so rigging, documentation and spares stay consistent. XIAORAN builds core rigs and also a compact crawler crane range; its spider crane manufacturer page lists the capacities and footprints that suit confined ground. Whichever route is used, write the lifting plan down before mobilisation: rated capacity at the actual working radius, ground bearing under the outriggers, and a clear path for every module from the transport point to the collar.

Crew, water and consumables on a restricted site

Access drives everything downstream. If the rig arrives by hand, so do the water, the casing, the drill rods and the core boxes on the way out. Plan the daily consumable tonnage against the carry distance and the number of trips a crew can realistically make, then size the program to that number rather than to the rig’s best-case metre rate. Water supply is the usual bottleneck: recirculation, a sump and low-volume circulation let a carried rig keep drilling when a fresh-water haul would otherwise stop it. Add the return leg for core — a full core box is not light, and a program that ignores it slows down exactly when the core gets good.

Frequently asked questions

How much can a crew realistically move per trip on a trail?

It depends on grade, surface and the crew’s conditioning, which is why module weight should be specified from the carry plan rather than from a catalogue number. Break the system down until no single module exceeds what two people can move safely over the worst section of the route, and test that section before the rig is ordered.

Can a man-portable rig still use wireline coring?

Yes, but it changes the rod string and the handling weight. Wireline removes the need to pull the full string for each core, which reduces the total moving effort across a deep hole even though the head assembly is heavier. Confirm the winch, ropes and inner tubes match the wireline size you plan before you commit.

What depth is realistic for a hand-carried rig?

Realistic depth is set by the rod size and the ground, not by a single number, and it falls as core diameter increases. For anything past a shallow hole, compare a hand-carried system against a walk-in crawler rig on a cost-per-metre basis for the whole program, and ask the supplier for the rating in writing at your target core size.
