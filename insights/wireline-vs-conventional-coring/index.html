Wireline vs Conventional Coring: Which Method Fits the Program

How wireline and conventional coring differ in rod handling, cycle time and hole conditions, plus how to choose between them for a core drilling program.

Published November 21, 2026

Wireline coring retrieves the core barrel through the drill string on a wire rope, while conventional coring pulls the entire rod string to recover each core. The difference decides cycle time, crew effort and which rig class the program actually needs.

What actually differs between the two methods

In wireline coring the inner tube is winched up through the rods, so the outer rod string stays in the hole. In conventional coring the whole string comes out for every core run. Everything else — crew size, cycle time, depth economics, even hole stability — follows from that single mechanical difference. Neither method is better in the abstract; the right one depends on depth, core diameter, ground conditions and how much time the program can afford per metre.

Cycle time and the depth where the difference bites

Shallow programs rarely separate the two methods on cost. At 30 metres, pulling the string takes minutes and a conventional setup is simple and cheap. The gap opens with depth: trip time grows with the number of rods, and every additional rod adds handling, thread care and the risk of a joint problem. On a 300 metre hole, the difference between a wireline run and a full trip is measured in hours per core, and hours multiply across a campaign. This is why wireline dominates exploration programs that go past a few hundred metres, and why conventional coring survives in shallow geotechnical work, large-diameter sampling and sites where the rod string is short enough that the extra hardware is not worth it.

Core size, recovery and ground conditions

Wireline systems are built around standard wireline sizes — BTW, NTW and HTW — with the core diameter fixed by the size you run. Conventional coring accepts a wider range of barrel designs, including large-diameter and specialty barrels, and gives more freedom over bit type and barrel length. In broken or swelling ground, the inner-tube retrieval in wireline coring can reduce the time the hole sits open, which often helps recovery. In very soft or cohesionless material, neither method recovers core without the right barrel and fluid plan, and a conventional barrel with a proper core catcher is sometimes the more controllable choice.

What the difference means for the rig

Wireline needs a wireline winch with enough rope capacity for the target depth, plus a rod string and head assembly matched to the wireline size. Capacity is the specification to check first: the FDX200 carries 300 m of wireline and reaches 200 m on NTW, while the FDX400 carries 500 m and reaches 400 m on NTW. Same transport footprint, same 7 m mast, same 1,200 N·m torque — but the deeper rope capacity is what lets the larger machine finish a wireline program without re-rigging. A rig quoted with a wireline winch that is short on rope capacity will stop before the hole does.

Matching the method to the commercial picture

Write down the target depth, the core size, the metres planned and the crew available before comparing quotes. A wireline package costs more per rig — winch, inner tubes, head assembly — but reduces hours per core. A conventional package costs less up front and is easier to run with a small crew on shallow holes. On programs that mix shallow geotechnical holes with deeper exploration, the honest answer is sometimes two configurations rather than one compromise. Ask for both price lines in the same quotation so the comparison is like for like.

Frequently asked questions

Can a conventional rig be converted to wireline later?

Only if the rig has a wireline winch option with the rope capacity for the depth you plan. Adding the winch is the easy part; the rod string, head assembly and inner tubes must match the wireline size as well. Ask the supplier for the conversion scope in writing rather than assuming a bolt-on winch is enough.

Does wireline coring always give better core recovery?

No. Recovery depends mainly on the barrel, bit, fluid and ground condition. Wireline helps by reducing the time the hole stands open between runs, which can limit disturbance in broken ground, but a poorly matched barrel will lose core in either method.

How much faster is wireline in practice?

It scales with depth rather than being a fixed percentage. On shallow holes the saving is small; on a 300 metre program the difference in trips per shift is substantial. The useful comparison is hours per core run at your target depth, not a headline speed claim.

What core sizes should I plan for?

Decide from the sample requirement, not from the rig. BTW, NTW and HTW give progressively larger core, and the size you pick fixes the rod, barrel and bit. Check that the rig's quoted depth for that size still covers your target — depth capacity falls as core size increases.
