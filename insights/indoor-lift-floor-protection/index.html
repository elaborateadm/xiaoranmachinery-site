Protecting Finished Floors During Indoor Spider Crane Lifts

How to plan floor loading, outrigger pads and protection materials when a spider crane works inside completed buildings..

Published November 19, 2026

Why indoor lifts stress floors differently

Outdoors, a crawler crane spreads its weight over soil or compacted ground. Indoors, the same machine concentrates load through four outrigger feet onto a structural slab designed for people, furniture and pallet trucks — not point loads. The governing question is not the crane's tonnage but the pressure under each outrigger foot, and whether that pressure, after distribution, stays within the slab's verified capacity.

Work out the numbers before entry

Get three figures from the crane datasheet: machine operating weight, maximum outrigger reaction (usually at maximum radius with rated load) and the standard outrigger foot area. Divide the peak reaction by the contact area of the pad you will actually use. A 5 t machine whose peak reaction is 35 kN on a 0.04 m² bare foot applies roughly 875 kPa — far above typical raised floor systems, and marginal even on some structural slabs. A 0.25 m² pad brings it to about 140 kPa, which most ground-floor slabs tolerate.

Protection materials compared

MaterialTypical thicknessLoad spreadingBest use
Hardwood crane mats50–100 mmHighBare ground, rough slabs, outriggers at full load
HDPE / UHMWPE pads20–40 mmMedium-highFinished slabs, repeated repositioning, wet areas
Steel outrigger plates10–25 mmHigh (small area)Point loads under each foot on sound concrete
Rubber / felt interlay5–15 mmLowFinish protection only, always under a rigid layer

Values are indicative. Pad sizing must be confirmed against the slab's allowable load by the site engineer.

Match the pad to the slab, not the crane

The correct pad is the one that satisfies the slab engineer, not the biggest one you own. Ask the building's structural engineer for the allowable distributed load, then size the pad so peak outrigger pressure, after spreading, sits comfortably below it. On suspended floors, locate outriggers over columns or load-bearing walls where practical, and never span a pad across an expansion joint or between different slab levels.

Protect the finish, not only the structure

Structural safety and finish protection are separate problems. Hard mats protect the slab; they can still scratch terrazzo or dent resin floors. Layer the system: a soft interlay (felt or rubber sheet) against the finished surface, then the load-spreading mat on top. Replace the soft layer wherever it picks up grit — trapped debris under a mat grinds the finish during every slew cycle.

Plan the travel route as carefully as the standing position

Most floor damage happens while driving, not lifting. Crawler tracks concentrate load on two narrow bands; route the crane over the same line, use temporary track protection on long runs, and sweep the path before every transit. Mark the standing position and travel route on a simple site plan so the operator — and the site supervisor — agree before the machine passes the doorway.

Frequently asked questions

Do I always need outrigger pads on an indoor slab?

If the calculated peak outrigger pressure exceeds the slab's allowable distributed load, yes. Even when it does not, pads reduce pressure concentration and protect the finish — most indoor lift plans specify pads regardless.

Can the crane drive on raised access flooring?

Generally no. Raised floor systems carry very low point loads. Either remove the floor panels along the route and lift from the structural slab, or demonstrate with calculations that the system, its pedestals and the slab below can carry the crane loads.

Who confirms the floor is safe to lift from?

The building's structural engineer or a qualified engineer appointed by the site. The crane supplier provides weights, reactions and foot areas; the site confirms capacity. Get it in writing before the crane enters.
