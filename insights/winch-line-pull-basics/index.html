Winch Line Pull vs Rated Capacity: What the Numbers Mean

One number describes the rope, the other describes the lift at a working radius. Reading them as equivalent is how lifts get overloaded.

Published November 24, 2026

Winch line pull and crane rated capacity sit side by side in catalogues and quotations, and they are routinely read as two ways of stating the same limit. They are not. One is a force on a rope, measured under a specific drum condition. The other is a maximum hook load published for a machine at a stated radius, boom length and support arrangement.

Quick answer: Line pull is the force a winch can exert on its rope, quoted at a defined drum layer and usually at the most favourable one. Rated capacity is the maximum hook load a crane is published to lift at a stated working radius and configuration. A line pull figure cannot be substituted for a lifting rating, and a rated capacity cannot be read as drum pull.

What a line pull figure actually describes

A winch line pull is a force at the rope, derived from drum torque divided by the effective drum radius. Because the drum radius grows as rope builds up in layers, the same winch produces less pull on the second layer than on the first, and a bare-drum figure is always the most favourable number a manufacturer can quote. This is why a catalogue that lists one line pull value without saying where on the drum it was measured has told you less than it appears to.

Reeving changes the figure again. With two parts of line, the tension in each rope leg for a given hook load is roughly halved, and the drum sees a lower tension but travels twice the rope length. A single-part hoist means the rope carries the full hook load plus the mass of the hook block itself. Any comparison of line pull numbers is meaningless until the drum layer and the reeving are identical on both sides.

Where a rated capacity comes from

A crane rated capacity is read from a load chart at a working radius, for a specific boom length and a specific support configuration. The published figure already accounts for the machine's stability and structural limits, and it is a hook load: the mass of the object plus the lifting gear hanging below the hook. The number changes as the boom extends and as the radius increases — on the same machine, the fully extended boom carries far less than a short boom at the same radius. Our guide to reading a load chart at real working radii covers how the curve is used in a lift plan.

That is the structural difference between the two numbers. Line pull describes what a drum can pull on a rope in one moment. Rated capacity describes what the whole machine is published to lift, in a defined geometry, with the load chart as the governing document for the individual lift.

Winches that lift, and winches that pull

A hoist winch on a crane is part of a lifting appliance: it works with brakes, a rope that is inspected and a rated load that is charted. A recovery, towing or vehicle winch is rated for intermittent line pull on terrain, and that figure says nothing about holding a load suspended, drum brake rating or overload protection. Treating the second as if it were the first is the common misuse.

If a quotation gives you a single figure, ask two questions before using it: is this a rope force or a hook load, and at what radius or drum layer was it measured? A supplier who cannot answer both has not given you a lifting rating.

What to send when you need the numbers compared

For a compact lifting job the inputs are consistent: the hook load including lifting gear, the working radius, the lift height, the access route and transport width, the surface the machine stands on, the site power supply and whether the machine must work indoors. Send those and the comparison becomes a model recommendation with a chart attached.

Frequently asked questions

Can I compare a winch line pull figure directly with a crane rated capacity?

No. Line pull is a force exerted on the rope at a stated drum layer and reeving; rated capacity is the maximum hook load published for a machine at a stated radius, boom length and support configuration. They describe different quantities, so one cannot be substituted for the other. Use the load chart for the machine and the specific lift case.

Does a higher line pull mean a better lifting machine?

Not necessarily. A line pull figure says nothing about the load chart, the drum brake rating, the hoist rope specification or the stabiliser arrangement — the four things that actually decide whether a lift is permitted. Two machines with the same line pull can have very different rated loads at your working radius. Compare the load chart rather than the winch headline.

What should I request in a written quotation for a compact crawler crane?

Request the exact model, the load chart for the delivered configuration, the rated load at your working radius, the hoist rope and reeving specification, the stabiliser arrangement and spans, the power options and the accessory list.
