Scissor Lift Pre-Operation Checklist: What to Inspect Before the Platform Goes Up

Working height is only the first number to check when purchasing a scissor lift. Platform load, access dimensions, floor conditions, travel requirements and charging or engine support determine whether a model fits the job.

Published November 20, 2026

Why the checklist comes before the work order

Most scissor lift incidents trace back to conditions that a five-minute walk-around would have caught: a deflated tyre changing platform tilt, a hydraulic weep that becomes a drift, an emergency lowering valve that was never exercised. A fixed pre-operation checklist converts those checks from memory into routine, and it gives the site supervisor a dated record when clients or auditors ask how the machine was maintained. The sequence below is ordered the way an operator actually walks the machine — ground, wheels, structure, power, function, work zone.

Ground and surface checks first

Before touching the machine, walk the area where it will stand and drive. Look for floor openings, expansion joints, ramps above the rated gradeability, grease or oil films that reduce tyre grip, and overhead obstructions — cables, ducts, door headers — against the platform's working height. Indoors, confirm the slab type matches what the lift was selected for; a machine chosen for ground-floor concrete cannot simply be moved onto a mezzanine without re-checking the slab capacity.

Machine walk-around: structure and hydraulics

With the platform lowered, inspect the scissor stack for bent links, cracked welds, missing pins and damaged slide pads. Check tyres for cuts and correct pressure, and confirm the pothole guards (where fitted) move freely and return to position. Look under the chassis for hydraulic leaks — a wet line is a failed line. Battery terminals should be tight and corrosion-free, and the charge level confirmed before the first lift of the day, not after it stalls at height.

Controls and function tests

Every function test runs from the ground controls with the platform empty: raise, lower, steer, drive creep, and the emergency stop on both control panels. Then test the emergency lowering valve — the function that matters most when the main system fails — and the tilt alarm by briefly driving across a deliberate, safe slope if the site allows. An operator who has never seen the emergency lowering work under calm conditions will not trust it under pressure.

Close the loop with paperwork

Record the completed checklist with date, machine serial, operator name and any defects found — and what was done about them. A defect that stops the machine stays stopped until it is fixed; a defect that does not stop operation still goes in the log so the service partner can track it. When the same fault appears twice in a month, the machine needs a technician, not another checkbox.

Frequently asked questions

How long does a proper pre-operation check take?

A trained operator needs about five to ten minutes for the walk-around and function tests. The first check of the day is the longest; repeat checks after transport or battery swaps can be shorter but are never skipped.

If the lift worked fine yesterday, can we skip today's check?

No. Conditions change overnight — a slow hydraulic weep, a tyre losing pressure, a battery left on float. The check is daily precisely because most faults develop between shifts, not during them.

Who is allowed to sign off the checklist?

The trained operator assigned to the machine that day. The supervisor countersigns when the site's rules require it. Whoever signs must have actually performed the checks — a pre-signed sheet is worse than none.
