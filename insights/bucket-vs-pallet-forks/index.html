Bucket vs Pallet Forks: Getting More From One Machine

Which of your jobs is volume-limited and which is weight-limited — and how load centre and material density decide what one compact loader can actually carry.

Published November 23, 2026

Most compact loader buyers start with one attachment and find a second job within the first month. The choice is usually posed as bucket or forks, but that framing hides the answer: the real question is which of your tasks is volume-limited and which is weight-limited.

Quick answer: a bucket suits loose bulk material, where bucket volume normally runs out before the machine does. Pallet forks suit unit loads, but hold the load further forward, so the same mass is harder on the machine. Size the bucket to the material and machine width, and the forks to the heaviest load at the furthest load centre you handle.

The two jobs are limited by different things

A bucket job is loose material: sand, gravel, topsoil, aggregate, grain, wood chips. The machine fills the bucket to its rated volume, and whether that volume overloads the loader depends on material density, not bucket size: two buckets of the same size can differ sixfold in mass between dry wood chips and wet gravel.

A fork job is a unit load: pallets, bagged goods, crates, pipes, drums. The mass is fixed by what is on the pallet, and the geometry differs, because the load is carried out in front of the attachment plate rather than tucked against it. Plan the fork job from the load centre and the bucket job from material density.

Load centre: the number that changes the usable rating

The rated load published for a compact loader is a machine-level figure, measured in a defined configuration at a defined load centre. A bucket carries its material close to the machine; a fork set holds the pallet out in front. The further forward the load centre sits, the less the machine can safely carry — not because the hydraulics cannot lift it, but because leverage and tipping risk rise.

That is a geometry effect, not something engine power resolves. Measure from the attachment plate to the centre of your normal pallet and quote that distance with the pallet mass; we will confirm the permitted load at your load centre, and the attachment mass, in the written quotation. How the rated figure sits with bucket volume is covered in our guide to rated operating capacity and attachment weight.

For light material the bucket volume is the limit. For heavy, wet material the machine is the limit, and a bigger bucket makes it worse.

When you need both tools on one machine

Running a bucket and forks on the same loader is normal. They do not compete for the same hydraulic circuit, because most pallet fork sets are a fixed tine assembly that mounts on the coupler and needs no auxiliary flow.

  • Attachment mass is part of the load. Every attachment consumes part of the rated load before any material is picked up, and a fork set consumes it further from the machine.
  • Match bucket width to the work. A bucket wider than your trailer or doorway is a liability on a tight site, and extra width means extra mass.
  • Check powered tools separately. Grapples, four-in-one buckets and sweepers need auxiliary flow, pressure and controls to match the machine — see our guide to attachment hydraulic flow and machine size.
  • Confirm the mounting interface. Machines and attachments do not share one universal coupler across every model and year.

Frequently asked questions

Can I just fit a wider bucket to move more material?

A wider bucket increases the volume it holds and usually the mass of the tool itself, and that mass is deducted from the usable load before any material is picked up. So the widest bucket that fits a coupler is not automatically right. Confirm the width and mass for your model and check the material mass against the published rated load.

Are pallet forks hydraulic?

Usually not. A standard pallet fork set is a fixed tine assembly that mounts on the loader coupler and needs no auxiliary hydraulics. Powered tools such as grapples and sweepers need confirmed auxiliary flow and pressure, plus controls to match. Flow, pressure and permitted load vary by model, so request the specification for your machine.

How do I know which load centre to quote?

Measure it on the load you actually handle: put a normal pallet on the ground, measure from the face that meets the attachment plate to the centre of the load, and quote that distance with the pallet mass. If pallet sizes vary, quote the heaviest load carried furthest forward.
