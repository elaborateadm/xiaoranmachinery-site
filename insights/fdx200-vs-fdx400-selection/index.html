FDX200 vs FDX400: Choosing Between Two Wireline Core Rigs

Compare the FDX200 and FDX400 wireline core rigs on depth capacity, weight, pullback, feed force and transport size to pick the right one for your program.

Published November 21, 2026

The FDX200 and FDX400 share a transport footprint, a 7 m mast and 1,200 N·m of torque. The decision comes down to depth capacity, 500 kg of extra weight and how much pullback the ground demands.

The short answer

Choose the FDX200 for exploration and geotechnical holes up to about 200 m on NTW where transport size and machine weight matter. Choose the FDX400 when the program runs past 200 m, needs 400 m of NTW capacity or 480 m on BTW, or when broken ground calls for more pullback and feed force. Both machines travel at the same dimensions, so site access is not the deciding factor.

Where the two rigs are genuinely identical

On paper the shared numbers are striking. Both use a 7 m mast, drill at 45–90°, carry 900 mm of mast compensation, have a 114 mm hydraulic clamp option, climb 30°, travel at 2.5 km/h on rubber tracks, and share the same transport dimensions of 5,400 × 1,800 × 2,300 mm and the same working footprint of 3,850 × 1,800 × 7,000 mm. Both deliver 1,200 N·m maximum torque at up to 1,400 rpm and both use a 15 kN winch with 6 mm rope at 110 m/min hoisting speed. If your site access, transport truck and drill pad are already sized for one, they are sized for the other.

Weight, ground pressure and site logistics

The FDX400 weighs 5.3 t against 4.8 t for the FDX200 — a 500 kg difference on the same track footprint. On firm ground that is a minor change. On soft ground, on temporary platforms, or on a pad built over fill, the extra half tonne changes how the tracks behave after a week of traffic. Both machines are rubber-tracked with the same dimensions and the same 30° gradeability, so the practical question is ground bearing capacity at the drill site, not transport.

Pullback and feed force in difficult ground

The FDX400 carries 16 t of lifting force and 8 t of propulsive force against 14 t and 6 t on the FDX200. That margin matters when the string starts to drag: collapsing formations, swelling clays, long rods in a deviated hole, or a barrel that needs to be worked free. On clean, stable ground at 150 m the FDX200 has enough margin. On a 350 m hole in broken ground, the extra 2 t of pullback and 2 t of feed is the difference between a controlled pull and a stuck string.

Fluid, power and the rest of the package

The FDX200 runs a Quanchai engine at 76 kW and ships with a BW90 mud pump; the FDX400 uses a Yuchai at 75 kW with a BW90 or BW100 option. The larger pump pairing on the FDX400 is not an accident — deeper holes and larger core sizes need more circulation volume, and a pump that is marginal at 200 m becomes a limiting factor at 400 m. Both machines carry 300 m or 500 m of wireline respectively, which is the figure to check against your target depth before anything else in the quotation.

When to step up to the FDX600

The FDX600 reaches 650 m on BTW, 600 m on NTW and 300 m on HTW with 92 kW, 1,600 N·m and 800 m of wireline capacity. If your program regularly runs past 400 m on NTW, or you need HTW core at 300 m, the FDX400 will be working at its ceiling. Moving up a class costs more machine but avoids a rig that finishes the hole at its limit every day.

Frequently asked questions

Do the FDX200 and FDX400 use the same transport truck?

Yes. Both share transport dimensions of 5,400 × 1,800 × 2,300 mm and the same 7 m mast, so a truck and permit arrangement that works for one works for the other. The FDX400 is 500 kg heavier, which matters for axle loading rather than for dimensions.

Is the extra 200 m of NTW capacity worth the price difference?

It depends on your deepest planned hole, not your average. If most holes finish at 150 m but one in five needs 380 m, the FDX200 will stop short on those holes and you will either re-rig or turn the work down. Price the two machines against the cost of an incomplete hole, not against each other.

Can the FDX200 drill a 300 m hole with a smaller core size?

Depth capacity rises as core size falls — 260 m on BTW against 200 m on NTW for the FDX200. Whether that reaches 300 m depends on the size, ground conditions and rod string, and must be confirmed against the datasheet for the exact configuration rather than extrapolated.

Which rig suits heli-portable or remote programs?

Weight and module size drive that decision more than depth. Both machines are rubber-tracked at the same footprint, and the 500 kg difference matters when lifting capacity is limited. Ask for the module weights of the actual configuration, not only the machine total.
