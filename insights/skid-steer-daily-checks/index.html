Daily Walkaround Checks for Skid Steer Loaders

What to inspect before the first start of the shift, which findings are stop-work items, and how the checks change across tracked and wheeled models.

Published November 23, 2026

A compact skid steer loader rarely fails all at once. It fails in small ways first: a track that has started to walk on its sprocket, a lift cylinder weeping at the gland, a coupler pin that has taken a set. None of these stop the machine on the day they appear; they stop it two weeks later, mid-backfill, with the spare part two weeks away. A ten-minute walkaround before the first start of the shift is the cheapest reliability tool on a compact site.

Quick answer: walk the machine before you start it — undercarriage and ground first, then fluids and leaks with the engine off, then attachment and coupler, and only then start and test the controls. Any wet hydraulic fitting, frayed track edge, or coupler that does not lock positively is a stop-work item.

Why a short machine needs a fixed checklist

A compact loader is short and narrow by design, which is why it works within a metre of a trench edge and against a finished wall. Its view forward is partly blocked by the lift arms and the operator structure, and the attachment sits directly in front of the machine.

Low operating weight and a short wheelbase also mean small changes matter quickly: a soft tyre, a slack track or a leak that has wetted one side of the machine shifts its behaviour before it shows up as a fault. A fixed sequence, run in the same order every shift, makes a skipped step obvious.

Before the key: the walkaround order

Do the first four steps with the engine off: a running machine hides leaks, noise and movement.

  • 1. Ground and undercarriage. Check the ground beneath the machine for fresh oil or coolant. On a tracked machine look at both tracks for cuts, embedded wire, loose pads and a track that has moved off-centre on its rollers — tension behaviour is covered in our guide to tracked loaders on soft ground. On a wheeled machine check tyre cuts, pressure and wheel nuts.
  • 2. Fluids and leaks, engine off. Check hydraulic oil, engine oil, coolant and fuel at the level points, then look at cylinder glands, hose ends and fittings for fresh wetness. A dusty fitting is normal; a fitting wet to the touch is not.
  • 3. Attachment and coupler. Confirm the pins, wedges or locking mechanism are fully seated and locked, the couplers are pushed home with the collars locked, and no hose chafes on the arm.
  • 4. Structure and guarding. Check lift arm pins, tilt linkage, bucket cutting edge and any loose guards.
  • 5. Then start. Idle, listen, and read the instrument panel and warning lamps before moving. Test lift, tilt and travel slowly in a clear area, and confirm the parking brake and interlocks.

Checks by system, and when to stop work

The escalation column matters more than the check column: most operators spot a fault, and it becomes a failure because it was tolerated for another shift.

Escalation triggers are general guidance. The operating limits and repair tolerances for your machine are those in the manual supplied with the unit.

The walkaround is not the service schedule

Daily checks are operator-level and take minutes. Greasing intervals, track tension, filter changes and fastener torque belong to the service schedule, and the tolerances are model-specific: a track tension for a 1.9-tonne machine is wrong for a 1.1-tonne one.

If the operator and maintenance manuals are not in the handover pack, ask for them before the machine ships. Items needed at the first service interval — filters, grease type, spare pins — should be named in the written quotation so they can be ordered with the unit.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a daily walkaround take?

About ten minutes on a familiar machine, and longer in the first weeks of ownership or after the machine has stood idle. The value is in the fixed order: if it never changes, a skipped step is obvious, whereas a check done from memory varies with the person and the weather.

Should the engine be running when I check the hydraulic oil?

Follow the procedure written for your machine. Some level checks are specified with the engine stopped, others with the system at operating temperature, and the reading is false if the condition is wrong. Overfilling aerates the oil and blows seals, so it is as damaging as running low.

Do I need to write the check down?

Yes, in three lines: date, hour meter reading, and anything found, even if the action is only monitor. A short log turns a vague complaint into a diagnosis and supports a warranty discussion. Keep it with the machine, not the site office.
