Loading a Skid Steer on a Trailer: Ramp and Tie-Down Rules

Deck rating, ramp angle, nose weight and four-point tie-down — the checks that keep a 0.9–1.9 t compact loader stable on the road.

Published November 28, 2026

A skid steer is short, heavy for its footprint and has no suspension to soften the way it sits on a deck. Those three facts explain most trailer incidents with compact loaders, and speed is rarely the cause. The causes are a deck rated for the machine with an empty bucket, a ramp steep enough to tip it at the transition, and two straps over the lift arms of a 1.9 t loader.

Quick answer: load to the trailer's payload figure, not its deck length. Add the attachment mass to the published operating weight, keep nose weight at 10–15% of gross trailer weight, and use a ramp no steeper than about 15°. Tie down four points on the chassis, lower the attachment to the deck, and re-check after the first 10–20 km.

Width catches people: a 1,240 mm machine needs a wider deck and longer ramp than a 1,045 mm one. The measuring method for gates applies to trailers too — see our narrow access checklist.

The trailer arithmetic to do before you load

Three plate numbers decide everything: gross vehicle weight rating (GVWR), the trailer's own weight, and the payload that results. Machine, attachment, bucket contents and tools all have to fit inside it.

Nose weight is the other half. Aim for 10–15% of gross trailer weight on the coupling: too little and the trailer sways at road speed however well the load is strapped, too much overloads the rear axle of the towing vehicle and lifts its front axle. Move the machine in 100 mm steps and check on a weighbridge, not by eye.

Ramp angle and the transition that tips machines

Ramp angle is rise over run, and it is easy to underestimate: a 600 mm deck with a 1.8 m ramp is already 18°.

Deck heightRamp lengthAngleVerdict for a compact loader
600 mm1,800 mm18.4°Too steep for routine use; bucket will scrape
600 mm2,400 mm14.0°Workable with the bucket 100–150 mm clear
600 mm3,000 mm11.3°Comfortable, good for tracked machines
800 mm2,400 mm18.4°Too steep; use a longer ramp or lower deck
800 mm3,000 mm14.9°Acceptable on dry ground with clean ramps

Angles calculated as arctangent of deck height divided by ramp length. Surface and tyre pressure matter as much as the angle.

Two habits make a marginal ramp safe. Keep the heavy end low: travel up with the attachment near the ground and never with a loaded bucket, which raises the centre of gravity exactly when the machine is pitched nose-up. Watch the breakover where ramp meets deck, because a sharp transition lifts the front off the surface and a skid steer then slews sideways.

Tie-down: four points, chassis only

The loader's weight is not what holds it on the deck; the tiedowns are. Use four points, two forward and two aft, in a crossed pattern so the machine cannot move forward, back or sideways. Anchor to the chassis or designated tie-down points, never to the lift arms, the bucket, the tilt linkage or a hose.

Chain and binders or rated ratchet straps are both acceptable with a marked working load limit. Many jurisdictions require the aggregate limit of the tiedowns to be at least half the cargo weight, and prudent operators use more; confirm the rule where you tow. Lower the attachment flat, apply the parking brake, engage any arm lock, and stop after 10–20 km to check every binder.

One trailer, several machines

A trailer is bought for a fleet, not one machine, so its rating must suit the heaviest thing that will sit on it. A 2,800 kg payload trailer that carries a 1.3 t loader comfortably is at its limit with a 1.9 t loader and attachment, and cannot carry a 3 t compact crane at all.

Where the work needs a loader for muck and a compact crane for lifting, compare weights first. XIAORAN builds both, and as a spider crane manufacturer we publish transport weight and folded dimensions per model, so one deck rating can be checked against both machines.

Pre-departure check

  • Ramp pins and surface. Both pins home, ramps dry, no mud or ice on the deck.
  • Four tiedowns tensioned and the attachment lowered to the deck.
  • Coupling and electrics. Latched and pinned, breakaway cable connected, lights working.
  • Height and width. Total height checked against the lowest bridge on the route.

None of this replaces the towing vehicle's limits: check rated towing capacity, ball or pin rating and gross combination mass, and confirm brake and breakaway requirements locally.

Frequently asked questions

Can I tow a skid steer on a 3,500 kg trailer?

Often yes, after the arithmetic. A 3,500 kg gross trailer weighing 700 kg empty leaves 2,800 kg of payload; a 1,892 kg loader, a 400 kg attachment and 300 kg of tools total 2,592 kg, with 208 kg to spare. Check the plate, the operating weight and nose weight before loading.

Should a skid steer be loaded forwards or backwards?

Load in the direction that puts the heaviest end over the axles and gives 10–15% nose weight. The engine usually sits at the rear, so forward loading puts that mass ahead of the axles — confirm on a weighbridge. Reverse loading is used when the ramp or deck length makes forward loading scrape the bucket.

Do I need brakes on the trailer?

Requirements depend on jurisdiction, trailer gross weight and towing vehicle. Many markets require trailer brakes well below the combined weight of a loader and trailer, plus a breakaway system. Confirm the rule for your country and test coupling, lights and breakaway cable before every trip.
